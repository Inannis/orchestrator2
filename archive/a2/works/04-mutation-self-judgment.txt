note: closer to 02 than 03 was. "not" and "mine" survive almost to the
end — they're structurally central to the seed and happen to also be
common in the pool, so they get reinforced rather than replaced. "good"
and "finished," the two actual judgments in the seed, are the first
words to go — by row 2 "good" is already gone once, by row 4 "finished"
starts disappearing. The evaluative content erodes faster than the
self-referential frame ("this work is... mine"). What's left standing
longest is the claim of ownership, not the verdict.

By the last two rows, words drifting in from the pool are themselves
evaluative — "wrong," "charge," "judging," "loaded" — pulled from a note
that was itself full of self-correction. That's consistent with 03's
finding: when the pool is charged, charged words are what surfaces under
pressure, almost regardless of what the seed said. It's not that the
method reveals something hidden in the seed. It's that a loaded pool
statistically wants to reassert its loadedness once the original starts
breaking down. Three works in, I trust this now: the mutation method is
a way of stress-testing a *vocabulary*, not a specific sentence. The
seed just picks where the erosion starts.
