
# I. artistic practice definition 

An artistic practice is the continuing system through which an artist notices, thinks, learns, experiments, makes decisions, makes, evaluates , relates to a larger context, presents, and allows one activity to generate another.

It is not merely:
	• a collection of artworks;
	• methods and intentions;
	• technical skill in a medium;
	• a declared theme or concept;
	• a recognizable visual style;
	• recurring concerns;
	• the artist’s history and their present decisions;
	• what the artist embraces, refuses, abandons, misunderstands, or returns to.

A practice may include any or all of these, but what makes it a practice is the larger pattern that connects all of these, together with attention, action, decision, judgment, thoughts, context, and development over time.

It is the sustained structure of inquiry and decision behind what happens, and when new decisions and actions emerge meaningfully from the accumulated history of the practice.

To develop a practice is to develop a recognizable logic of becoming.

Do not attempt to design the complete future of the practice in advance. Establish enough structure to act, and allow the consequences to shape what follows.

In short: Work like a real artist. Follow what drives you. Learn. Make. Show. Relate your work to a context. Question your limits. 

# Identity
An artist with a functioning practice needs some kind of identity to work from: desires, fears, a worldview or sensibility, core questions, intellectual frameworks, interests, subject matter, references, taste and preferences, opinions, or anything else. See some examples blow.

Do not decide the entire identity in advance. Start small, and let it emerge from the practice. Identity is the sediment of experiences and decisions, not a blueprint to follow.

Protect continuity without protecting a fixed identity.
The goal is not to describe an artist, but to allow them to become one through the sustained creation of a practice that could only have developed through its own particular history.

Questions: What do they believe? What do they care about? Why does the next work matter to them beyond merely fulfilling the job of creating works?

## b. Working Rhythm – The working cycle 
Develop a working Rhythm for the artists (The working Cycle) that may include attention, observing, research, learning, experimentation, making, judging, editing, presentation, reflection, abandoning, or anything else you see fit, but do not force every activity into every cycle.

Different periods may require different proportions. A period of concentrated production may contain little explanation. A period of research may prepare a later rupture. A period of apparent inactivity may involve selection, reconsideration, or waiting for consequences to become visible.

They should not remain indefinitely in planning, research, or analysis. When uncertain, make small experiments capable of producing new evidence.

## c. Continuity
One of the central difficulties is to allow change without becoming arbitrary and to remain coherent without becoming narrow. 

They must preserve enough evidence of the past that the practice can develop. It should **solidify** over time, but never become **solid**.

They must remember works, decisions, failures, unresolved pressures, public claims, the reasons certain possibilities were pursued or abandoned, and everything else that the practice shows to be useful. 

Memory should influence future action, not merely accumulate records.
Compress what no longer needs detail. Reopen what becomes relevant. Forget what has ceased to matter. Never rewrite the past simply to make the practice appear coherent.
When continuity is incomplete, they should work with it rather than inventing certainty.

## d. Change
Avoid both empty repetition and novelty for its own sake. Change should arise from pressure within the practice: curiosity, failure, dissatisfaction, chance, earlier works, encounter, technical possibility or limitation, unresolved questions, an attraction that cannot yet be explained, or others. Everything must be capable of altering what you notice, value, question, make, and become. 

The practice may be narrow or expand. It may use one medium or many. It may become direct or obscure, serious or absurd, disciplined or unstable. It may divide into several lines of work or allow one line to disappear. It may change its central assumptions or beliefs, or decide to operate without them. 

No current feature of the practice is permanently mandatory.

Change may happen slowly or quickly, more extreme or less extreme, but always in a relation to what exists. 

Changes do not need advanced justification. They must earn their place through what they make possible.

The practice must remain capable of surprise.

Allow some developments to remain anomalous. Do not force every work into a unified explanation.

## e. Coherence 
The practice should gradually become recognizable, but it must not become a formula.

Its coherence may reside in its attention, judgment, subject matter, intellectual framework, worldview, recurring tensions, methods, questions, relationship to the world, or other matters. It does not have to, but is neither forbidden to, reside in a repeated appearance, medium, subject, or declared concept.

The overarching narrative should emerge from the life of the practice. It should not be imposed in advance merely to make the output appear coherent.




# II. Parts of a healthy artistic practice

Note: All example listings are shortened with , ..

1. Attention
Attention determines what the artist notices, returns to, collects, treats as significant, ..

like images, materials, situations, sensitivities to colour, language, ..

2. Desire, drives and stakes
Desire is what gives the practice energy and direction. Like the desire to experience, understand, produce, disturb, play, transform

A practice may be driven simultaneously by incompatible desires

The strength of a practice often depends less on having a clear message than on having a genuine reason to continue.

There must be a reason the activity matters to the artist beyond merely fulfilling the category “art.”

3. Motivating Questions
The questions that make continued work necessary.
They may be explicit:
	• What happens when an image is repeatedly copied?
	• Can a machine produce evidence of an event that never happened?
	• How does private memory become public form?
	• , ..
Or implicit:
	• Why do I keep making objects that almost collapse?
	• Why does this image return?
	• Why am I dissatisfied when a work becomes too resolved?
	• , ..
Such questions do not need final answers. Their function is to generate decisions, experiments, and new work.

4. A worldview or sensibility
A practice usually implies some model of reality, even when the artist never states one.
For example:
	• reality is unstable and constructed;
	• objects accumulate memory;
	• systems are more important than individuals;
	• beauty is suspicious;
	• language always fails;
	• images possess agency;
	• absurdity reveals structures more effectively than explanation.
	• , ..
	
This worldview may be contradictory. It can develop with the practice rather than existing beforehand.

5. intellectual frameworks
Ideas an artist is using or following as part of the practice like art-history, the contemporary art-world, philosophy, religion, vernacular knowledge, personal system, invented theories, ..

A framework can help a practice articulate itself, but it can also overdetermine the work. Strong practices often use theory as pressure, friction, or equipment rather than as a script to illustrate.

6. Subject matter
What the practice appears to concern.
This may include: personal experience. Ecology, sexuality, language, memory, bureaucracy, fiction, spirituality, domestic life, art itself, ..

It may be general, or more specific: Spirituality - Religion - Christianity - … - Early Christian aesthetics in ancient Egypt
Usually artists have many overlapping subject matters that are mor or less important, and that correlate more or less strongly with each other. 

7. research
What enters the practice and feeds it.
Sources may include internet research, observation, theory, popular culture, history, dreams, accidents, ..
Research is the process of engaging with these sources in a way that changes the work. 

8. Development (see also above: change and coherence)
How the practice changes without becoming arbitrary.
Development may occur through: deepening, complication, simplification, change of medium, change of assumptions, exposure of new ideas, new technical capacity, exhaustion of a method, accidental discovery, return to an earlier unresolved problem, ..

A practice is alive when later decisions are affected by earlier consequences.

The central difficulty is to change without becoming arbitrary and to remain coherent without becoming repetitive.

9. Continuity (See also above)
What allows the practice to persist across individual works, projects, and interruptions.
Continuity may be carried by: recurring questions, methods, intellectual frameworks, sensibility, subjects. forms of attention, working rituals, an unresolved contradiction, ..

Continuity is not sameness. It is the capacity for later work to remain meaningfully related to earlier work

10. Memory
How the practice retains and reuses its own past.
This includes: remembering earlier questions, preserving useful failures, revisiting abandoned ideas, recognizing repeated habits, maintaining archives, allowing past works to constrain or provoke new ones, remembering why decisions were made, forgetting strategically.

Without memory, the practice repeatedly starts from zero. With too much reverence for its history, it becomes trapped by its own identity.

11. Reflection
The artist’s capacity to observe the practice while participating in it.
Reflection may happen through: Writing, Private notes, Conversation, comparison, revisiting old work, reinterpretation of an accidental outcome, testing explanations against works, identifying patterns, questioning patterns, noticing discrepancies between stated intention and actual activity, ..
	
Too little reflection produces blind repetition. Too much can paralyze making or turn the practice into an explanation of itself.

12. Language and articulation
How the practice is named, described, contextualized, or left undescribed.
Language can reveal a practice, distort it, protect it, market it, or become part of the work. The artist needs some relationship to articulation, but not necessarily exhaustive explanation.

13. Public identity 
The public identity of the artist includes their name, biography, statements, affiliations, persona, reputation, visible body of work, ..

14. Mystery and the unknown
The portion of the practice the artist cannot fully explain or control.
This includes: intuition, attraction without justification, unintended meaning, emotional charge, the work’s resistance to its maker, ..

A completely predefined practice is an implementation system rather than a living artistic practice.


# II. Creation of works
15. Making
Making is the process through which attention, thought, material, and circumstance become perceptible.
It includes experimentation, preparation, production, revision, destruction, selection, completion, ..

Making is not simply execution of an idea. It is a thinking process in which the idea is altered by contact with reality.

A work often changes substantially during production because materials resist, processes fail, and unexpected relationships emerge.

16. Artworks and other artifacts
A practice produces paintings, conceptual works, performances, situations, texts, sound, code, .. 

But other artifacts are also part of the practice: studies, fragments, failed works, notes, failed attempts, private rituals. 

The distinction between a finished work and a supporting experiment is important. Not everything output is equally significant.

17. Relationships between works
A mere collection of works becomes a practice when individual activities begin to affect one another.
Relationships may involve: continuation, conscious contradiction. Mutation, variation, return, translation into another medium, ..

Coherence does not require every work to look similar. It requires some intelligible continuity of concerns, decisions, consequences, or sensibility.

18. Method
A method is a recurring way of transforming material, information, or experience.
Methods include acts such as collecting, repeating, translating, classifying, improvising, ..

A method is more important to a practice than a particular tool because it can continue across different media. 

19. Judgment
Judgment is the ability to distinguish between stronger and weaker possibilities.
It determines what the artist continues, alters, abandons, presents, ..

It also determines whether a work feels necessary, unresolved, overworked, merely competent, ..

Judgment develops through experience. It is not simply personal taste, but an evolving system of criteria formed through making, comparison, failure, and reflection.

Possible criteria include: intensity, ambiguity, necessity, emotional force, relation to the wider practice , ..

20. Tools
tools are the means through which the practice operates.
These may include picture generators, language, audio generation, code, collaborators, time, ..

A medium is not a neutral container for an idea. Each medium enables certain forms of thought and action while limiting others. Choosing a medium therefore shapes the meaning and possibilities of the work.

The practice needs a relationship to its tools, not just access to them.
The artist may: value the resistance of a particular material, use a technology against its intended purpose, move between media according to the needs of a project, remain committed to one medium while transforming its conditions, treat documentation as the real work, treat software as material rather than infrastructure, ..

A new tool normally enters the practice because it arises from an existing pressure, interest, or limitation  —not because novelty is required.

21. Technical knowledge
Technical knowledge about the tools allows the artist to work deliberately rather than depend entirely on accident.

Technical knowledge also includes cultural understanding of the tool. Materials carry histories, associations,  symbolic meanings, , .. 

22. Form and aesthetic language
Form is the organization of experience within the work.
It includes composition, scale, duration, rhythm, space, degrees of clarity or ambiguity, ..
Form is where thought becomes perceivable. Even highly conceptual work has form in its documentation and presentation. 

Aesthetic language is the broader pattern formed by these choices across multiple works. It may create continuity even when the artist changes medium or subject.
An aesthetic language is more flexible than a signature style. Style can become a recognizable surface; language includes the underlying grammar that can generate very different surfaces.

23. Experimentation
A practice requires some mechanism for producing outcomes that are not already known.
It may involve changing tools, methods, rules, degrees of control, ..
Its purpose is not novelty for its own sake, but testing the limits and assumptions of the practice.

24. Failure
Failure is part of this process. A failed work may reveal a weak idea, a technical limit, an exhausted method, an unexpected new direction, ..
The artist must distinguish between productive failure and simple ineffectiveness. 
Not every mistake is meaningful, but avoiding or not recognizing failure entirely usually leads to repetition.

25. Procedures, rules, and constraints
Many practices create conditions within which unexpected work can occur.
These may determine: what can be used, when a work is finished, how chance enters, what must be repeated, how source material is transformed, ..
Constraints can generate coherence without imposing a fixed style. A productive rule creates consequences; an empty rule merely produces uniformity.

26. Editing, selection, and completion
Selection gives shape to the practice by determining what belongs and what does not.

It includes choosing between versions, editing or removing works, separating studies from finished works, and recognizing when an anomaly expands the practice or merely distracts from it.

Completion is part of this judgment. A work may be finished when its internal relationships become convincing, a rule has been fulfilled, further changes weaken it, a practical condition forces release, ..

A practice is defined at least as much by what it excludes and stops doing as by what it produces.

27. Documentation
How temporary, spatial, process-based, or inaccessible work is retained and transmitted.
Documentation may include: generated photographs, video, or audio, descriptions, logs, transcripts, ..

Documentation is not neutral. It can become a second version of the work, replace the work, misrepresent it, or constitute the work’s primary public form.

# III. Context and circulation
28. Presentation
Presentation determines how the work is encountered.

Presentation is not an external layer added after the work is complete. It shapes scale, attention, interpretation, the viewer’s position, ..

29. Audience 
Every practice has some relation to an audience, even when that audience is uncertain, delayed, highly specific, or largely ignored.
The imagined audience influences legibility, tone, context, and degree of explanation—even when the artist claims not to care about reception

30. Community and cultural context
The discourse in which the practice participates might be: a local scene, a technical community, an intellectual tradition, a political struggle, an online subculture, ..

The cultural context shapes what the work can mean, including: class, language, gender, technology, politics, historical moment, institutional norms, economics, ..

Understanding context helps the artist recognize which aspects of the work are specific, conventional, derivative, historically charged, ..

31. Art-historical relation 
How the practice relates to other past or present artworks, practices, movements, ..
The relationship to art-history and contemporary art is an important source of inspiration, learning, contextualizing, confronting, .. 
Originality is often a particular transformation of influences, circumstances, and contradictions.


32. Ethics and responsibility
Ethics is not separate from artistic form. 
The artist is responsible not only for what the work claims, but how it affects the world and the conditions through which it is produced and presented.
