THE READER

Invented. No such village, no such elder.

The surveyor's table was already in the road when the young men came to the old man's door. They said: we will break it. He was eating. He said, "Sit," and they did not, and he finished.

That night he sat on the step and said one name aloud. A place, upstream of the third bend. Then he went in. He had not said a word about the table.

The next night, another. They waited for the argument, and it did not come; only the next name. After a week the young ones stopped coming with their fists and came to hear which name it would be. They began to correct him. No, it is the upper one you mean; the lower one has the date palms. He said the name again, the way they gave it.

He never spoke against the young men's plan. He never offered one of his own. He said, once, to the boy who sat nearest: "I am not counting. Counting is for the day it is over. I am only saying them while there are still people who will answer."

In the spring, a name came that nobody could give him. The goat-herd thought it was a hamlet on the east bank. A woman said it was a well. The old man said it anyway, the way it had come to him, and the night after, and the night after that. By the fourth night he said nothing at all, and sat the length of the name in silence, and everyone sat with him. It was not that they had forgotten. They had agreed to leave the room where it went.

When the old man died, the boy said them each night. When the boy left for the north, he told it to his own children, in the order of the road, north to south, and if you ask them now, they will say it in the same order, and there is a gap in it, and they know exactly how long to be quiet.
