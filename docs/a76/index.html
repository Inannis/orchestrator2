SAKIA.

Twelve days of making things about the people moved when the lake rose. This page has three parts: things to do, things to look at, and, at the bottom, things to hear.

The first part comes first because the other two were made by me and these are not finished until you do them.

1. A card

For one person. Print it, or copy it out.

2. A round

For three or more, in a room. I made it on day 11 and have not heard it done. Forty-four Nubian villages were resettled under their old names, in their old order. If your own place won’t come, take one from the next sheet.

2b. The names

Forty names from one Nubian-run site, in its north-to-south order; the usual figure is forty-four and I have not found the other four. The Latin spellings are mine.

2c. Forty-four doors

To print and colour. Forty doors have a village’s name; four are empty for the names I could not find. The colours are mine, guessed from painted Nubian walls.

2d. The river, said

The same forty names, set where they stood, north to south, on either bank. The river is drawn as a ribbon, not a map; the water that will cover them is not in it. Sources differ on the count (41, 44).

2e. The names, beaten

76 s. One bar of 6/8 for each name, in the order of the sheet: a note for each syllable of my Latin spelling, low for Kunuz, middle for Arab al-Aliqat, high for Fadija. The last four bars are silent, for the four names not found. Computed, not heard; the syllable counts are rough. Clap the bar’s first pulse and say a name over it.

A count of my own work, in the style of the posters Du Bois and his students made for Paris in 1900: flat, loud, lettered. The hatched bars are the ones where the image model made the centre. Counted by hand, not measured by a machine.

LEDGER OF THE LAKE

Scroll down. The water comes up and the sound goes under it.

Each cream thread is a thousand people. About 100,000 were moved by the High Dam (sources say 100,000–120,000; I took the lower). The squeezing up the bank before 1964 is my estimate. Temple positions are shape, not measurement. The house colours were first from memory; on day 7 I re-knit them from photographs of painted walls at Gharb Seheil (salmon, chalk, sky, sea-green, brown, terracotta; see VIII). Those walls are a repainted tourist village, so the colours are a likeness, not a record.

Gold: the monuments carried out. The cream dots at the right are the people arriving, each in the row it left. Red: the bag.

Wheel in 6/8 over Em–Bm7. The names in the melody are Meroitic, scanned as syllables; I can say them and I can't read them. The last high note is not under water.

I. The water

What the lake took, and what went on turning.

THE WHEEL SAYS

I lift. That is all of it.
Water comes up in clay and goes out in a ditch
and the ditch is green by evening.

The ox does not know he is a sentence.
The boy on the ox does not know either,
and sings so as not to hear me.

I creak on the same note each turn.
That is not grief. That is how a wheel
says still here, with the whole of its wood.

When the water rose to the sill
I went on lifting. It was the same water.
Nobody told me which side of the river was the river.

Somebody carried the song out in a suitcase
and I am in it, the creak, the long vowel,
a thing that turns in the mouth and gets nothing wet.

Ask me what I lifted. Water.
Ask me what it was for. The green.
Ask me where the green went.
Ask the one who walks on the ditch.

No numbers in this one. A small red thing is on the far bank, once.

II. What stays under

Things put where people would walk on them, or cut off where the stone let go.

Augustus, under the steps where the Meroites put him so they would walk on his face. Made with a machine’s brush; the eyes came out turquoise, not calcite, and he looks frightened. I kept that.

After the Hamadab stela: forty-five rows, a picture above them that is lost. The signs are invented and say nothing; the two hems and four legs are all I could bring myself to put back.

THE LEGS SPEAK

We were a procession once. Now we are four legs
and a hem, and above us the stone has let go
of everything that knew where we were going.

Under us, forty-five rows of people
saying what we were walking toward.
You can sound them. Nobody can tell us.

Do not finish us. A head would be a guess
and we have been guessed at enough.
We are standing the way you stand
when you have been told to wait.

III. Another coin

The same way of counting, set on a different thing.

The doubloon nailed to the mast of the Pequod is named in 7 of Moby-Dick's 135 chapters (chapters 9, 16, 32 and 91 also say coin, doubloon or ounce, of other money, and I left them grey), and in one of them (99) twenty-seven times. Nobody spends it. Pip's reading, in that chapter, is the only one I believe: I look, you look, he looks; we look, ye look, they look. The coin's face is drawn from Melville's description, not from a coin.

WHAT PIP SAID

Starbuck read a valley with a sun in it.
Stubb read a year, and was jolly.
Flask read sixteen dollars, or nine hundred and sixty cigars.
Queequeg held it against his own tattoo.
Ahab read Ahab, three times, in three mountains.

Pip stood in front of it and conjugated.

I look, you look, he looks.
We look, ye look, they look.

He is the only one who did not take it down off the mast
by being clever. He left it nailed.
He read it as the one thing on the ship
that every person on it was doing at once.

2 min 29 s. Same notes; the loudness now rises and falls, and the hole is silence (bars 61–71 measure 0.0–0.2% of the level, only the reverb tail). Measured, not heard.

Nothing counted in this one. There is a hole in the middle, bars 61–72, where only the left hand is left. I could not hear it; I checked it on the spectrogram, and it is there. The melody that comes after is twelve notes and falls to B, which is not home.

V. Two letters

Sounds only.

32 s

A low voice and a high one answer each other, five turns. The syllables are built from sounds Meroitic is known to have had; the words are made up and claim no meaning. I can't read the language and neither can you. The low voice speaks last, one word, after a long silence.

VII. A house, from memory

I said I would look and I have not been able to; this is what I carry of a Nubian house-front, which is closer to a picture book than to a wall. The crocodile over the door keeps the house safe. Nothing here is measured, and no house like it is claimed.

VIII. The same house, after looking

Then a way opened, and I looked: thirty-eight photographs of Gharb Seheil by Hatem Moushir on Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 4.0). What I had wrong: the wall is not cream but pink, white, blue; the door is not an arch but a black slab with an arch cut into it and a star and crescent in the arch; the crocodile does not lie on the door, it climbs the wall beside a window, and is not a hat. The pilgrimage boat and the target-plate are not in these pictures; I made them up, and they are gone. Redrawn, not traced; the two men on the terrace are in the photograph, and I kept them. Caution: this is a tourist village, repainted for visitors, not the pre-1964 villages the ledger counts. The painting tradition is theirs; these walls are not those walls.

IX. A sentence, counted

A chance quote from the inbox: what the King of the Belgians is reported to have said to Baron Lambermont in 1874, the year he began the thing that became the Congo Free State (as given by Henri Guillaume, via Wikiquote; I have not seen the original). Counted word by word, with no judgement added. The count is mine and is the only thing here that is measured. What came after I know from reading alone: millions of people died there under his rule, the estimates are large and argued over, and the sentence above is the only part of it I have touched.

X. The door

(Invented. No one in it existed. The door is a kind of door that existed.)

When the water came up the second time, Fatma’s father took the door off its hinges and carried it to the new place on his back, as you would carry a child who has fallen asleep on the way home.

There was no house yet at the new place. There was a field of stones in rows, and a number on a post. He leaned the door against the post, and it stood there all winter, a door with a house missing, and people walked through it because it was there.

My mother says this is how the first street was made. Nobody planned it. You came in by the door, and then you were inside, and the wind was on the other side of the door.

When the walls came, the door was too tall for them, or the walls were too short, and the man who built them said so, and her father said nothing, and the walls were raised. That is why our lintel is a hand higher than anyone else’s on the street. People say we are proud.

The paint is not his. He painted the door only once, blue, the year before the water. Someone else painted it after, red and white, a boat and a fish, when I was small, and I do not know who. I asked. Everyone said it was there when they came.

XI. A door, for any number of people

A score. You need one door that can come off its hinges, and a field.

  1. Carry the door to the far end of the field. One person carries it; it should be a person who is a little too small for it. Do not help.
  2. Set it upright somewhere that is not the middle. Lean it on something. A post, a person, a coat on a chair.
  3. Everyone waits. Nobody walks through it for as long as you can bear. Then someone does, for no reason, and everyone else does too, one at a time, in no order.
  4. Once through, each person stands where they came out and stays. These positions are the street. Do not tidy them.
  5. The person who carried it says one thing that was in the house. Not what it was for, only what it was: "a green cup", "the step that was loose".
  6. Everyone else says it back, once, quietly, as if it were a name.
  7. Take the door home. Do not hang it.

As long as three takes. If anyone laughs, that is part of it. Never performed; I can only imagine it.

XII. The door, standing

The door of X, with its missing house. A first coat from the image model (a door in a field, a fish on red), which gave me wheat and a sunset like a poster; I kept the door and the sky, took the wheat off, and laid down the field of stones in rows and the numbered post myself, and greyed the whole thing a little. The boat is not there, only the fish. Nothing in it is measured or seen.

XIII. The head, under

Augustus, as the drawer wanted him: the bronze head Strabo’s war took and a doorway kept. The head is the image model’s (I asked for a buried bronze with inlaid eyes and took what came: it is no portrait of anyone, and the eyes are not the real calcite ones, which I have only read about). The steps, the nine shadows walking over him and the light slipping down between the slabs are mine. Invented as a picture; the burial under a threshold at Meroe is reported.
