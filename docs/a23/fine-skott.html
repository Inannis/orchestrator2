Joky, Itemised

"A Songe of a Fine Skott" is in Fugitive Poetry, 1600–1878 (ed. J. C. Hutchieson), pp. 343–345. I drew it at random from Wikisource's random-page list and read it from the transcribed page scans. It is about a Scot who came south in James I's reign and is now dressed above his station. Each stanza is one item, was and is turned now. I set them as a ledger. The words are the poem's; the colour chips appear only where the poem names a colour.

Two things in the poem itself. The north is a cloth: stockings of "the Northern hewe", a jerkin of "the Northern Gray", and both sit on the old side of the ledger. And the last stanza turns the refrain. Through the stanzas it is By my Fay, &c., after an opening that ends Joky will prove a Gentillman; in the last, "yf this happ doe still indure", it becomes Joky will be noe Gentillman, and the king is asked to grant them no more.

What the source says about itself, and what I didn't check. Hutchieson's headnote says he chose this text from The Mirror (22 May 1824) over the version in Collet's Relics of Literature, as "more likely from the original", and that it was copied "verbatim et literatim" from the diary of John Sanderson, a Turkey Company factor, with a note on the leaf: "A Songe of a Fine Skott, given me by Sir H. Boyer", probably around 1607, no date on the page. I did not look at the manuscript, The Mirror or Collet. So the spelling is at least three hands removed and I can't say whose. The headnote's account of a royal proclamation of 10 May 1610 is also the 1824 writer's, and is later than the probable date of the song. I took that on trust and checked nothing.

Not looked up: whether "List" here means selvedge (my reading; a garter cut from a tailor's selvedge fits), what "Spruce" means as a cloth, whether "Skott" is a contemporary joke on the Scots or a plain word for a Scot.
