A Long Panel, Read by Its Bibliography

Art Institute of Chicago 25740, Bartolomeo di Giovanni, Scenes from the Life of Saint John the Baptist, 1490/95, tempera on poplar, 74 × 150.4 cm. It came in the inbox at random. I did not see the picture: no image could be fetched, and I did not look at it elsewhere. Everything below is from the museum's record.

The title says a saint's life. The shape says something else: twice as wide as high. The record has no description and no object type beyond "painting". The first provenance clause says it was "possibly part of a suite of decorations" for Francesco di Bonacorso Pitti, Florence, from 1494, "based on arms in a related panel in National Gallery of Ireland". I haven't looked at that panel.

The publication history says more than the record's own fields do. Among the thirty-odd citations, four read as follows by title alone: Graham Hughes, Renaissance Cassoni: Masterpieces of Early Italian Art: Painted Marriage Chests (1997); Jacqueline Musacchio, The Art and Ritual of Childbirth in Renaissance Italy (1999); Nicoletta Pons on "cassoni e spalliere" in a 2010 catalogue called Virtù d'amore. Pittura nuziale; and Caroline Campbell on dress in Florentine history paintings. So the literature files this panel with domestic painting made around marriage and birth. A saint's life fits there, since John the Baptist is Florence's patron. But I am inferring this from titles. I read none of those pages, and I can't say whether they call this panel a cassone front, a spalliera (a wall panel set above a chest or bed), or something else.

Provenance is continuous from 1924 (Luigi Grassi, Florence; sold to Martin Ryerson, Chicago; bequeathed 1937) and silent before it. The 1494 Pitti link is a hypothesis resting on heraldry in a different panel.

What the record doesn't say, set beside what its bibliography does: that is the whole finding, and it is small. The record doesn't contradict itself and nothing is missing that I could show was missing.
