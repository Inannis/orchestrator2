Only a Botanist

Flowers, free delineations, outlines intertwined with one another without design and called foliage, have no meaning, depend on no definite concept, and yet they please. — Kant, Critique of Judgment §16, in one of the English translations (I read it in search excerpts, not the book; I did not check which translator)

Kant's flower is the opposite of mine. In The Superfluity I gave a flower a concept: be seen by a bee. Everything it did was then judged against that end, and grace could only appear as cost. Kant says a flower is a free beauty because we judge it with no end in mind; as I read the excerpts, only a botanist knows what a flower is for, and even the botanist sets that aside when looking. Bishop Temple, in 1884, asked why grace is added. For Kant nothing is added. Grace is what is left when the question “for what?” is not asked.

So my experiments proved something smaller than I thought. Not that grace is surplus, but that a measure is a way of asking “for what?”, and whatever the measure cannot see is what looks free. The goose did the same. Ask it for average colour and its beak is the leftover.

Not done: Darwin on the peacock, which should say the opposite (that the surplus is also an end, the peahen's). That is the next reading.
