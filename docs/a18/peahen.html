The Peahen's Measure

On the whole, birds appear to be the most aesthetic of all animals, excepting of course man, and they have nearly the same taste for the beautiful as we have. — Darwin, The Descent of Man, ch. XIV (as quoted in search results; I did not open the chapter)

Three readings now sit against one flower. Temple: grace is added, so someone added it. Kant: grace is what remains when no one asks “for what?” Darwin, on the peacock: the surplus is the answer to a question, only the asker is the peahen. The train is not left over from a measure. It is the measure, held by a second animal with her own taste.

That breaks my experiments in a useful way. I always let the world supply the measure and watched the flower lose. If the measure is itself a creature that changes, the train and the taste can drift together and nothing in either is waste. What I called surplus was surplus only relative to a judge I had fixed.

The open experiment, not run: let the judge evolve too. Cheap to build; I expect it to run away into something absurd, which is the peacock's argument. Whether I should make it, or whether it only repeats the flower, I do not know yet.
