# The Cut Has More Than One Job

*By Mara Venn*

Rill’s *River Openings* begins with a useful contradiction: the cut is both a way through and a thing that keeps figures apart. Across five digital relief prints, cream channels cross red walls, rails, and thickets. Blue marks run along those channels like water, but also like the evidence of a second, imperfect impression. The work’s repeated vocabulary is plain enough to read at once; its interest lies in how that vocabulary changes its job from sheet to sheet.

In *The Current Will Not Join Us*, a broad pale ribbon winds through a brick-red band. Two small people occupy the dark ground above and below it, one much larger than the other. The title denies the easy conclusion that a passage makes a meeting. The figures share a view of the opening, perhaps, but not a scale or a place in the picture. The wall seems less like architecture than a strip laid across a dark field, and the current’s clean path gives the eye a route without offering a destination.

The neighboring print, *The Blue Passes Through*, puts a bird where the first work put a person. A pale channel crosses the wall; a broken blue bird shape hangs over it, while a brown bird remains below. That outline reads at once as a trace and a small failure of registration. It is more compelling as an absent mark than as a symbol with a fixed meaning. Rill says the image began with a chance photograph of birds in Virginia, then carefully distinguishes this invented scene from a record of their behavior or habitat. That distinction matters: the print borrows the grammar of observation while refusing the authority of documentation.

The second pair narrows the scale. In *The Rail Opens Twice*, two birds occupy separate gaps in a long red rail, the upper bird blue and the lower brown. Patterned leaves repeat behind them, while the rail’s twin openings interrupt that regularity. *Where the Rail Gives Way* makes the interruption larger: an oval of paper widens until it reaches the right edge. A bluebird stands on a broken lower rail, near the opening but not quite its guide. Here the page itself starts to behave like a landscape with an edge one can leave by. The series’ strongest formal move is this shift from a bounded hole to a channel that escapes the frame.

These are images about printmaking that were not made by printing. Their SVG matrices and PNG impressions simulate grain, paper, and misregistration; the maker is explicit that no block was carved and no sheet hand-pulled. That candor sharpens rather than cancels the tension. The designs borrow the tactile authority of relief printing—cut shapes, layered color, small offsets—but keep the surface digitally even. The imagined material process becomes part of the subject, especially in *Outside the Impression*. There, a straight cream sheet sits against transparency while broken blue and ochre lines continue past its edges. The print’s premise is that a mark may exceed its support; the PNG makes that proposition visible without pretending that ink has touched a mount.

The limits are close to the strengths. The palette and repeated motifs give the five prints coherence, but the controlled geometry can make their encounters feel pre-solved. Birds and people are tiny tokens against broad, carefully balanced shapes; they rarely disturb the design that contains them. In the river pair, the unprinted channel is so legible that ambiguity has to come from the title and the figures’ separation. The work is most alive when a form changes status—paper becomes water, a broken contour becomes an afterimage, an edge becomes an exit—not when its symbols merely recur.

A second page, *The Room Finishes Last*, offers a different kind of opening. Its score asks two people to clap softly in a resonant room, wait for each sound to disappear, move, and answer or stop. There are no counts or fixed route; after one easy breath without an answer, both leave in silence. The diagram’s paired colored waveforms suggest exchange, but the instruction gives the room the deciding part. This belongs to the old lineage of event scores and instruction-based art, though its modest occasion is less a puzzle than a way to listen to an interval. No performance is claimed, and the piece ends as a score until someone tries it.

The move from pictured passage to performed interval feels like a real extension of Rill’s concern. In the prints, an opening lets a trace cross while keeping witnesses apart. In the score, a sound crosses a room and may or may not be returned. The work is becoming less concerned with depicting the cut than with assigning it a duration: the pause before an answer, and the quiet that can remain after one.
