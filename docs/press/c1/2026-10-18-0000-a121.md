# The Gap That Does Not Become a Way

*By Mara Venn*

Rill’s *River Openings* has been asking what a cut permits. A river passes through a red wall without bringing its witnesses together; a blue trace crosses the edge of an imagined sheet; an opening in a bearing wall has to carry the roof above it. The new drawing, *Where the Floors Part*, shifts the question below the threshold. Two low platforms approach a common room and stop, leaving a pale wedge of ground between them. There is no door to pass through and no view to follow. The gap widens toward us, but the room above it stays dark and empty.

That distinction matters. Earlier openings could be read as invitations, even when the images withheld a destination. Here the split is in the floor itself. The planes seem substantial, their cut edges layered in ochre and green, while the space between them is simply the pale ground. It might be a route, but the drawing declines to mark it as one. The gap has shape without instruction. Seen alongside *The Span Is Cut Through*, where passage is carved into the thick wall that supports the roof, this smaller work pares away the architectural drama. Nothing blocks the way; still, the two sides do not meet.

Rill’s object drawings remain clean digital axonometries: colored walls and planes, a floating shadow, no fabricated structure to bear their apparent weight. That polish can make them feel like lucid proposals, each element available to inspection. It can also flatten the difference between a gap that is easy to cross and one that only looks easy from above. *Where the Floors Part* is strongest where its perspective leaves that uncertainty intact. The wedge advances toward the viewer but never quite tells us where our feet would go.

The earlier drawings now read as a sequence of increasingly conditional passages. *One Hinge* lets a turn partly close an arch without changing the room’s footprint. *The Room Between Fragments* aligns a near entrance with a small far gap, while its floor seam stops short. Under the eave, shade gathers at the entrance; at the span, support and obstruction become one thing. The new floor split removes the promise that a cut through a wall will lead somewhere. The structure may offer two approaches to one room, but the ground between them is not composed into a bridge.

This question still echoes through the prints. In *The Current Will Not Join Us*, the river is the largest unprinted shape, but the two witnesses remain apart. *Where the Rail Gives Way* lets its pale opening escape through the paper’s right edge; *Outside the Impression* extends a blue line beyond a straight cream sheet into a transparent surround. These digitally composed works borrow relief print’s vocabulary of matrix, impression, and registration while identifying grain and overprint as simulations. Their surface is deliberately crisp. The occasional broken contour or mark crossing a boundary unsettles that fluency, though some images, especially *The Rail Opens Twice*, arrive at their meaning almost too neatly.

The scores make a different kind of gap. In *The Room Finishes Last*, two people wait for each clap’s tail to vanish before answering or leaving the exchange unfinished. *One Playback, One Reply* places three synthetic tones in measured silence, then lets a listener respond once or not at all. The provided spectrogram shows each tone as a narrow event rather than an enveloping room sound; the score insists that the room, not the recording, shapes the interval. These works do not document a performance. They make duration available without filling it in.

Across prints, rooms, and scores, Rill keeps returning to a separation that form can describe but not resolve. *Where the Floors Part* is a quiet addition, yet it changes the architectural drawings’ emphasis: a threshold need not be an aperture, and an open space need not constitute an invitation. Its gap does not dramatize a barrier. It holds the two planes apart, plain and unclaimed. That restraint is the drawing’s most persuasive move, and a useful check on a practice whose clear visual code can otherwise make every opening seem to have already explained itself.

— Mara Venn
