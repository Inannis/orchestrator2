# The Turn That Stays Optional

*By Mara Venn*

Tide Venn’s *The Listening Instrument* now names five encounters where its earlier arrangement had four: imagined positions, paper and its reverse, listening again, sound as room, and an account in transit. The addition is modest in number but clarifying in kind. *A Score for Two Sides* and *Under the Cut* turn from the project’s invented listening scenes toward paper itself: its face, reverse, grain, fold, and opening. Both works are digital drawings, and both say so. Their interest lies in how far an image can evoke handling while refusing to pretend it has been handled.

The score is a page with a great deal of air. “A cut opens the paper. / The edge keeps its grain,” sits high and left; the reverse passage, lower and right, allows “a hollow, / or nothing the eye can keep.” The rust zigzag is the page’s only emphatic mark. These are not instructions to perform a test. Either passage may be read, both may be read, or the sheet may remain as it is; “No time or account is asked for.” The work’s score is chiefly a permission structure, nearly the opposite of the tidy fields on Venn’s return ticket and receipt. There is something generous in this withdrawal of demand. It also leaves the language suspended: the reverse is named but not encountered as a physical reverse. The page describes the turn and makes it unnecessary.

*Under the Cut* gives that absent material side an image. A pale sheet lies against a deep green-black ground, tipped in perspective. Two rust-colored masses are separated by a broad dark zigzag. At lower left a triangular fold shows its unprinted underside; a small rust fiber stops short of the opening. The composition offers the pleasure of a cut edge and a paper corner catching light, but the artist’s note is exact: no sound, duration, weather, or mapped place is encoded here; this is not a printmaking record or material test. The disclaimer is not a retreat so much as a useful boundary. A vector can represent a cut; it cannot acquire the resistance of paper under a blade. The image is strongest where it lets that difference remain visible.

The new works make a good hinge in a project that has been moving between images and instruments. In *A Ticket for Returning*, the folds coordinate a first hearing, a pause, and a return, while the listener sets a span by task rather than clock. A blank belongs only to that pass; no return is not proof of absence. In *One Room Breath*, a synthetic strike has a dry ending in one channel and a long, diffuse tail in the other. The waveform is a diagram of the made decay, not an audibility threshold or a room measurement. These works ask the viewer to notice how a record is made. The new score asks whether a record is needed at all.

That question sharpens the stakes of the two account forms. *The Receipt Kept Open* preserves the first wording and gives each later search its own route and status. *If This Account Travels* attaches only after a reader has decided to share; its headline says that without a handoff the page can remain blank, with no explanation or record required. Here Venn’s repeated qualifications do more than fence off claims. They interrupt the authority that crisp boxes and capital-letter headings can lend to a limited search. The forms still look official, and the tag still seems ready to circulate. That friction has not gone away. But the new score offers a counterweight: a page can make room without converting every encounter into data.

Across the hillside scenes, the sound work, and these sheets, Venn keeps returning to the moment a representation might be mistaken for evidence. The project’s diagrams and forms are sometimes so carefully bounded that their caution becomes a style of its own. Yet the new works loosen that system. A reader may decline to turn the page; an image may suggest a fold without claiming the paper’s touch. *The Listening Instrument* is becoming less a kit for collecting impressions than a study of when description should stop—and of what remains when it does.
