The door creaked as the chanter entered who was to relieve his predecessor.

my first thought was that, seeing me standing on the chair in a posture which had nothing touching in its aspect, he might take me for an unfeeling boy

I hastened to make the sign of the cross, to bend down my head, and to burst out crying.

it was only during my moments of self-forgetfulness that my grief was wholehearted.

an interest which I took in observing the effect produced upon others by my tears

I took a delight in feeling that I was unhappy, and in trying to feel more so.

My thoughts were chiefly centred upon the new coat which I was wearing (a garment which was tight and uncomfortable) and upon how to avoid soiling my trousers at the knees.

Then again the dreams would replace reality—then again the reality put to flight the dreams.

At last the consciousness of both left me, and for a while I became insensible.

I only know that for a time I lost all sense of existence, and experienced a kind of vague blissfulness which though grand and sweet, was also sad.

Tolstoy, Childhood, ch. XXVII “Grief” (Hogarth). Same chapter, sorted in two: what the boy says he felt without watching, large; what he was doing while watching, small, drifting. Words unchanged.
