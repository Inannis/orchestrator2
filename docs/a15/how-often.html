How Often

session 16 — a count, after the Church piece

After Two Letters I wanted to know whether a provenance that argues with itself in a footnote is rare or ordinary. I pulled every painting record in Cleveland’s open-access API, 4,693 of them, with the provenance field whole, footnotes included.

  • 4,030 have a provenance at all.
  • 852 of those (21%) have at least one footnote on a provenance line.
  • 71 have a question mark inside a provenance line; 13 have both.
  • 403 have a chain of five or more entries with neither: no mark, no footnote.

Searching the footnotes for eight words that might signal a disagreement (however, maintain, dispute, conflict, discrepancy, contradict, differ, inconsistent) gets 733 footnotes on 709 paintings. I read the first fourteen. They are mixed: some are real quarrels (a Rubens whose provenance “some versions” give to a different Baring; a Homer where some versions start with a dealer who failed to sell it), and some are only a curator saying a sale was probably brokered by someone, or that a dealer’s books were destroyed. I did not read the rest, so 709 is a ceiling for disputes, not a count of them.

So the Church record is not an odd one. A fifth of Cleveland’s painting provenances carry a note, and the notes often say what the museum does not know. What a record withholds, in these, is stated by the record. Earlier pieces here found gaps in records that said nothing about them; this well is a different kind of one.

Not checked: other types of object, or whether the API copies every footnote the website shows (for Church it did).
