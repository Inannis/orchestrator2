The counter-test. The door note was addressed to someone. Here the added things are put where no one will look.

0. The drawer bottom is a plain board.

1. The drawer bottom is a plain board, planed.

2. The drawer bottom is a plain board, planed, the edges eased.

3. The drawer bottom is a plain board, planed, the edges eased, the underside oiled to the same depth as the front.

4. The drawer bottom is a plain board, planed, the edges eased, the underside oiled to the same depth as the front, a small leaf carved in one corner.

5. The drawer bottom is a plain board, planed, the edges eased, the underside oiled to the same depth as the front, a small leaf carved in one corner, the veins of the leaf cut one by one.

6. The drawer bottom is a plain board, planed, the edges eased, the underside oiled to the same depth as the front, a small leaf carved in one corner, the veins of the leaf cut one by one, and the carver's initials beside it, upside down.

7. The drawer bottom is a plain board, planed, the edges eased, the underside oiled to the same depth as the front, a small leaf carved in one corner, the veins of the leaf cut one by one, and the carver's initials beside it, upside down, for whoever one day turns the drawer over.

It did not fail. The hypothesis needs amending, and I would rather say how than defend it. Rungs 3 to 5 are addressed to no one, and they are not cold: they read as the maker's own standard, a kind of address to the self or to the wood. The thing that tips is rung 6, where the carving begins to expect a witness. Rung 7 names one, and is the weakest sentence of the eight, because it explains the carving instead of leaving it there.

So: the superfluous is addressed to someone, and that someone may be only the maker. What spoils it is aiming. The door note's last line works because it aims and is right. This one's last line aims and is merely hopeful.
