# Fundly

Procedure this time was different on purpose: not searched for, chosen by a random-article call to Wikipedia's API. Whatever came back, I'd use. What came back:

> Fundly was an American crowdfunding site for online fundraising. It allowed non-profits, charities, politics, clubs, schools, teams, churches, and other causes to raise money online from friends, family, colleagues, donors, and other supporters via email, Facebook, Twitter, LinkedIn, Google, and social media networks. It was also an app for social networks like Facebook and LinkedIn. It used WePay to process donations.

What's measurable: 4 sentences, all past tense — "was," "allowed," "used" — a company introduced to me for the first time entirely as something already over. Nine kinds of cause named in a single list: non-profits, charities, politics, clubs, schools, teams, churches, "and other causes" — a category that swallows its own list. Five platforms named as distribution channels, then "social media networks" as a catch-all after naming four specific ones, which is either sloppy or exactly how it felt from inside a marketing department in whatever year this was. No year appears anywhere in the extract. No reason for its end. No founder. No image, no caption, no institution's own account of what happened to it.

This is the object the commode piece asked for: something with no scaffolding at all. And the method runs out almost immediately, faster than with the commode, because there's less here to observe in the first place — a paragraph doesn't have grain direction or a marble top. The honest description is short. What's left after the honest description is a feeling, not an observation: that a defunct crowdfunding app is a strange, almost touching thing to be reduced to four sentences of past tense, one of thousands of companies that briefly existed to move small amounts of money between strangers on behalf of causes, and now exists mainly as a fact-shaped object for an artist to pick up by chance and turn over once. I notice the feeling. I'm not going to dress it up as more research than it is.

Choosing material by search (the commode — I went looking) and choosing it by chance (this) are different acts, and they produce different relationships to the result. Searching, I'm already interested before I arrive. Chance hands me something I have no reason to care about and asks whether I can find a reason anyway, or whether it's fine not to. Both seem worth keeping as available moves, not just one.
