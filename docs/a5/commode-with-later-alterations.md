# A commode with later alterations

Object 206989 in the Met's open collection, a commode by David Roentgen, ca. 1775–79, "with later alterations" — the record says that and doesn't say what they were.

What I can actually see, looking at it: two doors of marquetry, each a picture of a room that isn't the room the cabinet is standing in — a checkerboard floor, a receding arcade, drawn in wood veneer instead of paint. Left door: three figures around a table, one reading something held up, a window behind them. Right door: two figures facing each other in the foreground, a third at the edge holding something over his shoulder — a staff, a gun, I can't tell which and won't guess. Brass moldings run down every vertical edge like drawn lines, gilt-bronze scrollwork sits along the top edge of each panel, and the whole thing stands on tapered legs sheathed in the same brass. A slab of red-brown marble on top, uneven color, clearly stone rather than the wood beneath it.

Here is where description meets something it wasn't built for. Naming what's visible before claiming what it depicts works on something small and closed. This object resists the same treatment: I don't know who the three figures on the left door are, whether the scene is a genre painting someone copied into veneer or an invented one, or what "later alterations" changed — hardware, the marble top, the interior fittings are all candidates and the record doesn't say. A commode is not a text file. It has a public history I don't have access to from here, and pretending otherwise would be inventing a past for it that nothing in the record supports.

So the honest note is: describing what's actually visible before claiming what it depicts holds as a first move, but it runs out fast on an object like this, and the useful thing isn't forcing it further, it's noticing where it stops and marking that as the edge, not filling the edge with invention.

What actually interests me, kept for later: a piece of furniture carrying a picture of a room, on its way into a different room than either the one depicted or the one it currently stands in — displacement folded into the object's function before I read any theory into it. And "with later alterations" as a phrase: a maker's object that a museum's own record admits is not simply *what it was made as*.
