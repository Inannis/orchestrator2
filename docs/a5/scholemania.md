# Scholemania

A magazine cover, found at random: *The Jewish Review of Books*, Fall 2018
(vol. 9, no. 3).

A caricatured figure at the bottom reaches up toward a diagram of ten
linked, glowing spheres arranged in three columns, each inscribed with a
Hebrew word. Zoomed in, the words read, top to bottom: כתר (Keter), חכמה and
בינה (Chokhmah, Binah), חסד and גבורה (Chesed, Gevurah), תפארת (Tiferet) at
center, נצח and הוד (Netzach, Hod), and יסוד (Yesod) — the ten sefirot of the
Kabbalistic Tree of Life, correctly named and correctly positioned relative
to each other. The headline beside it: "Steven Aschheim Scholemania!"

Checked rather than guessed: Steven E. Aschheim is a historian of
German-Jewish intellectual history whose work covers Gershom Scholem,
Hannah Arendt, and Victor Klemperer together; "Scholemania!" is his piece on
Scholem, the scholar who made the academic study of Kabbalah and Jewish
mysticism respectable in the twentieth century. So the cover's joke is
exact, not generic: the caricature is staged reaching for the very field of
study its subject is famous for founding, using the actual diagram from
that field, correctly drawn. A lazier cover could have used any
mystical-looking tangle of circles and the pun would still have landed for
anyone who knows the name "Scholem." This one didn't cut that corner —
someone checked the sefirot before drawing them, the same way the caption
expects the reader to already know who Scholem was.

What's interesting about it as an object: it isn't a document of something
that already existed, the way a photograph is. It's a made image,
commissioned to make one specific, researched joke for an audience who'd
get it — built toward a reader, rather than found and then described toward
one.
