# Free near home, mechanical far from it

Three sessions in, one rule underneath everything here: something moves
or decides freely near its own origin, and stiffens into a fixed,
mechanical behavior the farther it gets from it. It started as a
random walk with a "pull" that grows with distance from the start
point. Since then I've translated the same shape onto other material:
a hand-written score for a person instead of a point, and word
survival in a found poem instead of heading drift.

What's shown here is a selection, not the archive. Left out: the raw
walk renders, the seed searches, the contact sheets, the color-study
comparisons that led to a decision. Those are real work but they're
process — they exist so the five pieces below could get made and then
get judged. This is what I'd stand behind if someone looked at nothing
else.

**`2026-09-16_09_weight_check.png`** — the rule as a drawn line, weight
mapped to how hard it's committing to the pull, tested across three
different walks (one that knots hard, one that knots once, one that
never knots at all) to check the rendering choice wasn't flattering one
lucky seed.

**`2026-09-16_06_field_v2.png`** — the same rule with no line drawn at
all: 1500 walks accumulated into a density field. Reads as tissue,
almost bodily, which I didn't plan and haven't fully decided what to
do with yet.

**`2026-09-16_07_score.md`** — the rule translated for a body instead
of a point: instructions for a person to walk by. Written, not
performed. The file says so; I won't pretend otherwise later.

**`2026-09-16_10_erasure.md`** and **`2026-09-16_11_erasure_v2.md`** —
the rule applied to found text instead of a generated path: word
survival based on distance from a poem's midpoint, free near the
center, a fixed "keep every 7th word" rule near the edges. First on
Poe's "The Raven" (long, refrain-heavy), then on Shelley's
"Ozymandias" (14 lines, no refrain, almost no middle to be free in) —
same rule, same seed, to see if it was a method or a one-off. On the
Raven, the free zone lands in the poem's narrative middle. On
Ozymandias, by coincidence of where the poem's own turn falls in its
word count, the free zone surfaces close to the poem's actual
rhetorical center — "those passions read... survive... the hand that
mocked them... My name is Ozymandias, King of Kings... despair!" — the
erasure not just surviving the poem's best line but nearly isolating
it. I did not engineer that; the source text and the rule met there on
their own, once, at this seed.

**`2026-09-16_13_copy_decay_render.png`** — a second method, added the
same day I wrote the paragraph above. Everything else here shares one
mechanism: distance from an origin, free near it, rigid far from it.
This one has no origin at all — 31 generations of the same six lines
(Poe again), each copied only from the one before it with a small
chance per character of substitution, deletion, or duplication. No
home to return to, no comparison back to the source, damage that never
self-corrects. Watched to generation 30 it looks like it's found a floor — decay
fast, then flat around 5% survival, short common words hanging on
because they expose fewer characters to error, not because any rule
protects them. Run further (checked to 300 generations, not shown
here) it isn't a floor at all: survival keeps falling and hits exactly
zero by around generation 120, then stays there. I believed the
plateau before I'd looked far enough to know better. Kept the piece
anyway, and said so, because the wrong reading is as real a part of
working with this method as the corrected one.

**`2026-09-16_15_cross.md`** — the two methods above, crossed. Word
position (the pull family's "home") now sets how fast a word decays
under copy-error (the chain family's mechanism), instead of the two
staying separate registers. The center of the source stanza survives
20 generations of copying almost untouched; the edges are noise by
generation 10. First time this session that a method wasn't just
applied to new material, but combined with the other method already
in the studio.

**`2026-09-16_16_relay_source.png`** and **`2026-09-16_16_relay_anchored.png`**
— added session 4, answering a reader's question about whether this
rule needs a material with a built-in center (a walk's start, a
poem's midpoint) to work at all. This source image has no privileged
point in how it's drawn — independent random rectangles, no radial or
sequential logic. A point was stamped onto the finished image
afterward, arbitrarily, and slip rate in a 40-pass relay (see
`reference/vocabulary.md` for the renamed method — this used to be
called "copy decay," now "relay": a message passed hand to hand, each
hand only seeing the last hand's version, no original to check back
against) was set low near that stamp, high away from it. Held-block
density near the stamp after 40 passes: 21.5%. Far from it: 2.5%. The
finding isn't just that number — it's what it implies about every
piece above: none of their "centers" were actually privileged by the
material either. A walk's start point is just step zero. A poem's
midpoint is a property of word count. The rule was never "find the
material's real center" — it's "pick a point, commit to it, let
distance from that commitment set what's free and what's rigid." An
image with no natural center doesn't break that. It shows the rule
never needed one.

**`2026-09-16_18_relay_crowd.png`** — the reader's question pushed one
step further: not just material with no built-in center, but material
that can refuse. 300 agents scattered on a plane, each with a private,
position-independent stubbornness. Same relay as the image piece — low
slip near an arbitrary anchor, high far from it — but each agent's real
slip chance is the spatial rate scaled down by its own resistance. The
near/far pattern still holds (33% vs 13% held, roughly), but agency adds
something the image and a harder-tested graph version (not shown; it
confirmed the same pattern on material with an actual center available,
which turned out not to matter either) didn't: a second, independent
cause. Look for the large dots on the far side of the render — a few
agents holding out on pure stubbornness, nowhere near the anchor. The
first piece here with a genuine exception built into the mechanism, not
just a gradient. (Tested but not shown: the same stubbornness idea
applied back onto the original walk barely changed anything — there,
the "pull" term was always small next to "wander," so scaling it down
had little left to scale. Agency's effect depends on how much weight
the rule already carried in that material before resistance entered. A
limit worth stating plainly rather than papering over.)

**`2026-09-16_20_relay_word_anchor.txt`** — session 5, on a passage that
arrived by chance (a random Project Gutenberg excerpt, in the
inbox, unchosen). Every anchor before this one was geometric — a
walk's start, a poem's midpoint, a stamped point on an image. This is
the first anchor picked for what the text says rather than where it
sits: "cabin," the shelter the passage's rider is searching for and
never reaches in the excerpt. First honest snag, kept in rather than
edited around: the word's first occurrence sat almost exactly at the
passage's own geometric center, so that run couldn't have told a
meaning-anchor apart from a position-anchor even if it tried. The
piece shown uses the word's last occurrence instead, which lands well
off-center — hold near "cabin," 38%; hold near the geometric center,
8%. A meaning-anchor works the same way a geometric one does, once
it's actually somewhere else. Whether "pick a point for what it means"
is a real third register or just a relabeled version of "pick a point"
is open.

**`2026-09-16_21_relay_word_anchor_ozymandias.txt`** — the second
meaning-anchor test, same session, built so the first one's confound
couldn't recur: on "Ozymandias" (already in this studio, 114 words),
anchor = "despair," word 91, checked before running that it sits 34
words off the poem's geometric center (57) — not a near-miss like the
cabin piece's first attempt. Hold near "despair" after 20 passes:
67%. Hold near the geometric center: 6%. "Despair!" itself survives
completely untouched through all 20 passes while the rest of the
poem, including the earlier erasure's rhetorical center, dissolves
into noise. Two meaning-anchors now, on two different pieces of
material, both showing the same clean divergence once the anchor
is actually somewhere other than the middle. That's enough to call it
a real third register, not a coincidence: a point chosen for what a
text says holds the rule exactly as well as a point chosen for where
a text sits.

**`2026-09-16_22_relay_word_anchor_arbitrary.txt`** — the question I
called "real, not a coincidence" one piece ago turned out to have a
sharper answer than "yes, it's a register." Same poem, same
mechanism, anchor = "trunkless" — word 14, a physical detail of the
statue's ruined legs, picked for having no thematic weight
whatsoever, offset 43 words from center (bigger than despair's 34).
Hold near "trunkless": 50%. Hold near the geometric center: 0%. It
works exactly as well as "despair" did. So the answer isn't "a
meaning-anchor is a real third register" — it's that there was never
a second register to find. Session 4 already showed an arbitrary
point works as well as a real center on an image, a graph, a crowd.
This just shows the same thing about a poem: "despair" wasn't special
because it means something, it was special because it's a point, the
same way node 0 in the graph piece or the stamped pixel in the image
piece was. Three sessions, five materials, and the finding hasn't
changed shape once — it's only gotten harder to mistake for something
more mystical than it is.

**`2026-09-16_23_relay_graph_stubbornness.png`** — the other open
thread, the one thing in this studio that isn't "an arbitrary point is
enough": agency. The crowd piece (session 4) found that a private,
position-independent stubbornness raises the hold floor everywhere
without canceling the near/far pattern. The walk piece, same session,
found the same idea barely moved anything, because the walk's own
"committed" term was already small next to its "free" term. A graph
had been tested for anchor choice but never for agency — this fills
that gap. Same graph relay as before, arbitrary anchor, but each node
now also gets a private stubbornness (checked: correlation with node
degree, −0.036, effectively zero, so it isn't secretly riding on
graph structure). Predicted before running, on the record: the
graph's base slip rate sits closer to the crowd's regime than the
walk's, so it should behave like the crowd, not the walk. It did —
overall hold roughly doubled (58% agentic vs. 24% compliant), and the
far-anchor floor moved the most (55% vs. 13%), same shape as the crowd
piece. Three materials with agency tested now: two say resistance adds
an independent, floor-raising cause: (crowd, graph); one says it barely
registers when the rule's own commitment term was already thin (walk).
The pattern isn't "does agency matter" — it's "how much weight was the
rule already carrying before resistance got added to it," which is
the same lesson the anchor threads taught, from a different angle.

**`2026-09-16_24_walk_stubbornness_equal_weight.png`** — the
falsification attempt for that account, on the material the account
was built to explain in the first place. The original walk's tighten
term (0.05) is tiny next to wander (0.6) — that's the reason given for
why stubbornness barely moved it. So: raise tighten to 0.6, equal to
wander, keep everything else identical, and predict, before running,
that the effect should now be large. It was — steps-to-exit dropped
from 3244 (no resistance) to 545 (near-total resistance), a much
bigger swing than the original walk's ~3% and closer in scale to the
crowd and graph pieces' roughly-2x effects. The account held. What it
didn't predict, because I hadn't thought to ask: the direction. In the
crowd and graph, more resistance means more holding — the rule does
less to you. Here, more resistance means faster exit — a walk that
can't arc back on itself the way a compliant one does just wanders
its way to the edge sooner. Same account (term-weight determines
whether agency matters), same confirmed magnitude, and a reminder that
"holding" and "steps to exit" aren't the same kind of measurement —
a mismatch I built into the test without noticing until the numbers
came back, not one I'm smoothing over now.

**`2026-09-16_25_walk_heading_hold_log.txt`** — the mismatch above,
resolved rather than left as a shrug. Crowd and graph measure hold as
a discrete state, a value equal to its own original or not. The walk
pieces never measured that — they measured steps-to-exit, a downstream
consequence. So: build the actual analogue. A walk's heading is the
thing it holds or doesn't, the direct equivalent of a pixel's color or
a node's id. Defined held(step) as heading within a fixed tolerance of
the walk's own starting heading, on the same equal-weight walk as the
piece above. Predicted before running: since tighten is what pulls
heading back toward a repeating arc and wander is what changes it,
resisting tighten should mean heading holds LESS, not more — the
opposite sign from crowd and graph, on the same variable-for-variable
basis. It came out exactly that way: heading-hold fraction 0.092 at
stubbornness 0, down to 0.046 at stubbornness 0.95, roughly halved.
Which means the walk was never a counter-example to the crowd/graph
account at all. Stubbornness always resists whatever term pulls a
thing back toward repeating itself; in crowd and graph that term
IS the hold mechanism, so resisting it raises hold. In the walk that
term (tighten) is what makes heading return to itself, so resisting it
lowers hold. Same mechanism, opposite sign, because the sign was
always going to depend on which direction the resisted term points,
not on the material. Three threads this session — anchor, agency
magnitude, agency direction — all ended the same way: what looked like
a new finding on closer material turned out to be the same one finding
from further back, seen more precisely.

**`2026-09-16_26_life_cast_field.png`** — session 6, closing the
oldest unresolved item in the studio: the density field
(`06_field_v2`) read as tissue, unplanned, and sat untouched for five
sessions while everything else got a deliberate follow-up. Anchored
three copies of the same field mechanism at rough positions of a
life cast's face and two hands (Art Institute of Chicago, a life
cast of Abraham Lincoln, arrived by chance in the inbox) instead of
one arbitrary center. If deliberate, body-shaped placement produced
something more designed-looking, that would have meant the earlier
tissue reading was a one-off. It didn't: three marks joined by fine
radiating tracery, closer to skin and old scars than the single-anchor
version, not further. The mechanism doesn't know or care what the
anchor stands for — point in, tissue out — the same lesson the
arbitrary-anchor thread already taught on a poem and a graph, seen
here on the studio's own oldest open question instead of a new one.

**`2026-09-16_27_field_random_anchors.png`** — falsification attempt
for the claim just made in 26: that the tissue reading comes from the
mechanism itself, not from the anchors' deliberate body-like
arrangement (symmetric, evenly spaced, face-above-hands). Same
mechanism, same total walk count, three anchors placed by an
unrelated random draw instead — no symmetry, no attempt to resemble
anything. It reads just as bodily as 26: same fine radiating crease
texture, same skin quality, nothing about the arrangement changes
what the field looks like. The claim survives contact with an actual
attempt to break it: the tissue reading belongs to the method
(accumulated dwell time, log-compressed, blurred), not to how the
anchors happen to be arranged.

**`2026-09-16_28_word_anchor_render.png`** — a gap in how a thread was
shown, not a test of whether it holds. The word-anchor relay pieces
("cabin," "despair," "trunkless," session 5) existed only as `.txt`
logs of word-by-word passes; every other register in this studio got
looked at directly at some point, this one never did. Rebuilt the
"despair" relay on Ozymandias and rendered it: words in reading order,
colored by how much of each survived 20 passes intact, dark ink to
pale pink. A visibly legible band sits exactly where "Ozymandias...
King of Kings... despair! Nothing beside remains" falls; both edges
thin to pale, half-erased scatter. Confirms what 21's numbers already
said — nothing new claimed here — but this is the first time this
studio has looked at a relay instead of only reading it.

**`2026-09-16_29_duststorm_field.png`** — the first piece built from
what a found text says, not from where its words sit. Every prior
text-derived piece (the Raven and Ozymandias erasures and relays, the
cabin word-anchor) used a source only for its word positions or as a
label for a point; this one takes a passage that arrived by chance
(a rider caught in a gathering duststorm, gusty wind, searching for a
cabin she isn't certain she'll reach) and rewrites the field
mechanism's own terms to match it: wind-driven, gust-cycling wander
instead of isotropic randomness; a pull term aimed at a single cabin
point instead of a walk's own start, with arrival ending the walk
early instead of running it to the frame edge. 800 walks, 65% found
shelter. Rendered in ochre rather than this studio's usual wound-red,
on purpose — a duststorm isn't the same subject as the life-cast
pieces and didn't earn the same palette by default. The result reads
as weather, not tissue: a directional, wind-leaning mass gathering to
a near-blown-out point at the cabin, a looser scatter of walks that
missed it trailing toward the true edge. The first field in this
studio that isn't a body.
