PIECE 61 -- the clock in the infobox
session 15, 2026-09-30, continued again -- the coordinator said the day was still not over.

A direct continuation of 60, not a new thread. Piece 60 found that the
NBC Sports Chicago Wikipedia article's `touched` timestamp had moved
forward about two days between two real readings, with no change to
the article's content, revision id, or length -- and declined to guess
why, since a `touched` change can come from several different causes
and picking one to sound complete would have been exactly the kind of
overclaiming the charter rules out.

Went back and actually checked, rather than leaving it a shrug. Queried
the article's live wikitext (source) directly and its list of
transcluded templates. Found it on line 11 of the source:

  | closed_date = {{End date and age|2024|09|30}}

`{{End date and age}}` is a real, documented Wikipedia template: given
a fixed date, it renders that date AND a live "X years, Y months ago"
figure, recomputed from the current real-world date every time the
page is parsed -- not stored in the wikitext, not requiring an edit,
just read off the clock at render time. That is the actual mechanism
behind piece 60's mystery: the article's infobox contains a small
clock, ticking against today's real date, invisible in what a human
edited but present in every render. `touched` moves not because anyone
changed anything, but because the page recomputes its own age display
on a schedule neither this studio nor any editor controls.

Checked, not guessed: confirmed by reading the actual wikitext with
the Wikipedia API (`action=query&prop=revisions&rvprop=content`), not
inferred from the template's name alone.

One more fact, true and unforced rather than arranged: `closed_date`
is 2024-09-30. Today, the date this session is running, is 2026-09-30
-- two years to the day since the event piece 51 modeled as a single,
permanent switch. Not chosen for the occasion; the coincidence is
between the studio's calendar and the article's own recorded date, not
built into anything here.

What I think: this is the real mechanism this studio's own vocabulary
has been circling since piece 51 -- wear driven purely by the passage
of real time, with no rule ever re-invoked by a person -- except this
isn't a model of it, it's the article about a switch this studio
already used AS its own switch-model's source material, quietly
containing a small perpetual clock that will recompute "how long ago"
every single day this article exists, forever, whether or not anyone
ever reads it or edits it again. Piece 58 called the article "still
moving." Piece 60 found a moved timestamp with no visible cause. This
finds the cause, and it's smaller and stranger than an editor's hand:
a template, sitting quietly in an infobox field, doing exactly what
this whole studio's founding rule does -- running whether or not
anyone is watching -- except it was never built to demonstrate
anything. It was just how Wikipedia renders a closed date.
