Piece 63 — still drying
=========================

Method: same as sessions 13-15 — a word from this studio's own
vocabulary ("rigid," from piece 01's founding rule) against the Met's
open collection API, today's date as the shuffle seed, declared before
looking, first actually-public-domain image-bearing hit taken as is
(the API's own isPublicDomain flag checked object by object, same
lesson learned session 13). Result: Albert Pinkham Ryder, "Landscape,"
ca. 1897-98, oil on canvas, Met 11978, public domain
(source: 2026-09-30_63_source_ryder_landscape.jpg).

Matched "rigid" by the same shallow lexical coincidence as every prior
hit this method has produced (53/54's "anchor," 55's "wear," 59's
"hold") — not by theme. Did not write that coincidence up a fourth
time; 53/54 already closed what there is to say about a search index's
blindness to earned vs. arbitrary matches.

What the image itself shows: a dense, fine web of cracks (craquelure)
running across the entire painted surface, visible at this resolution
without magnification — more cracking than typical for an oil painting
this size and age.

Checked rather than assumed: Ryder is one of the most documented cases
in American painting conservation of an artist whose own technique
built physical instability directly into the paint. He worked wet-on-
wet, adding new layers of paint, resin, and varnish before earlier
ones had dried, over periods of a decade or more per painting, and
used materials known to resist proper curing — candle wax, bitumen,
non-drying oils. The documented consequence, general to his body of
work: paintings that never fully dried even decades later, that
developed wide fissures and severe cracking (in at least one case,
"In the Stable," cracking bad enough to need conservator fill), and
that in the worst instances partially disintegrated — deterioration
that was visible within his own lifetime, and that he tried and failed
to arrest himself.

I have not seen a conservation report for this specific object (Met
11978) — no claim that it, individually, is still actively moving
today. What I can say directly: the craquelure visible in this
painting is consistent with, and looks like an instance of, that
well-documented general pattern, not an invented resemblance to it.

Why this is worth a piece rather than a passing note: every mechanism
family in this studio runs in discrete, counted passes — a for-loop,
a round number, a schedule (free/knot/release, climb/amnesty, 12-of-
20). Piece 51 built the one limit case with zero gradient, a pure
step-function switch. Ryder's paint is neither. It is a continuous
chemical process with no round count, no schedule, and — per the
documented record — in some cases still not finished a century after
it began, running whether or not a conservator, a viewer, or an artist
is watching. Not a record that moves while its event is over (58, the
Wikipedia thread) and not a record that moves with nothing behind it
(60's touched field) — this is the event itself, still running,
inside the object, long after the artist who set its terms is dead.
Piece 61 found the cause of a moving record and closed that thread
with an answer that turned out to be trivial, a rendering template. If
this thread is ever reopened, it would need to start here instead:
the one instance so far where nothing is rendering anything — the
material itself is doing the moving.

Not building a fifth mechanism family to formalize "continuous,
unscheduled wear." Every family in this studio has had a schedule or
a per-round test because a schedule is what makes a rule simulatable;
a real oil painting's slow internal chemistry has neither, and forcing
a round count onto it (the way piece 51 forced 20 rounds onto a step
function, honestly, by its own declared choice) would manufacture the
one thing that makes this case different from everything else this
studio has already built. Left as a checked observation, not a render.
