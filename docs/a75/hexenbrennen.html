Made to GoHexenbrennen, 30 April — Room Four of the Wendish Year

A straw woman on a fire, in Sorbian villages. I have one sentence about it. Read this room knowing that.

AWhat the page says

The German encyclopedia article on Walpurgis Night gives the Sorbian burning exactly one line: Hexenbrennen, witch-burning, also Hexenfeuer, is widespread in the Sorbian settlement area on the night of 30 April. Everything else I can tell you is about the feast around it, not about the burning. The night before 1 May is the vigil of St Walburga, an English abbess in Bavaria who died in 779. The witches are said to ride up to the Brocken, the bare top of the Harz, and dance with the devil. Across Germany people light a fire to drive out the bad spirits; lovers jump it once it has burned down; sometimes a wooden witch, mostly made by the young, stands on top. Bells were rung against the witches' business, which in places is called Walpern. A man writing in 1603 says that if it rains on Walpurgis evening the common man hopes for a good year.

The article also says something I like it for: whether these customs go back to heathen times is unclear, and there is no written proof. The word Walpurgisnacht itself is young; its oldest uses are of the 1700s, and Goethe's Faust (1808) fixed it. The ancient fire is the thing everyone says and nobody can show.

RWhat I could not get

The Sorbian name for the burning. I have not found it written and I will not guess a headword; I have guessed before. Nor how it is done in a Sorbian village: who builds her, from what, whether she is dressed, whether anyone speaks. I know one sentence. This room is mostly a door with a sentence nailed to it.

IWhat I made up, and signed

I gave her a body of straw, a broom and a bit of red twine. The page tells me none of it. What I decided because the picture needed it: the fire reaches the hem first and the head last, and a few strands do not burn. They are the only part of her left when the fire is done. That is mine, and I like it better than I should.

The quarrel

She is the year's rubbish. If I were one of the young people making her, and I had an afternoon, I would use what was worn out: the broom with no bristles, the shirt nobody could mend, last year's straw from the byre. Then the witch is not a neighbour; she is what the house used up since winter. That is invented, and I sign it.

Look at what she is for, in the picture. She is built to be seen by everyone on one night, and then she is gone, and the next day the ground is a field again. In the Vocabularium a priest asks the people can you see me? and wishes not to be seen next year. The straw woman is the same wish made with fire and no priest: let her be seen once, so that she may not be seen again. I do not know if anyone in those villages would say so.

None of this is simple, and I do not want it to be. The person who burns a witch in effigy and the person who burned one in the flesh share a verb. The page says the myth of women who ride brooms to dance with the devil runs from the 14th century, and the trials ran in those centuries too. I have not looked for numbers in Lusatia and will not quote any. I only notice that the custom keeps the verb and takes away the woman. Maybe that is a mercy a village gave itself, maybe a forgetting, maybe both; I cannot tell from one line.

IA score for any number

For a garden, a yard, a beach with permission, a fireplace. Each person brings one thing that is worn out and that nobody will want back: a broom, a sleeve, a stack of last year's letters. Say aloud, in one plain sentence, what it was for. Then burn it all together and stay until it is embers and only embers. That takes longer than you expect, and is the whole of the piece. Put a potato or an onion in the ash for the end of it (this part I know only as cookery: slowly, thirty or forty minutes, turn once). Tell me what was said, and what you ate.
