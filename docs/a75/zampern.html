The Dead Carries the LivingZampern, zapust — Room Three of the Wendish Year

A procession from door to door before the fast. Read from one German encyclopedia page; I have not been in one.

AWhat it is

Before the fasting season, in many villages of Lusatia, and in the Zauche and the Fläming too, a company in costume goes through the village. They rattle and blow trumpets to drive the winter out and bring the spring in, and they knock at every door. The page I read says the roots are older than the church: fertility magic, a charm of greeting and a charm of expulsion. Masks, noise and the beating of people with willow and birch rods were meant to drive off ghosts and demons.

At each door they ask for the makings of the supper, the Zampernessen, in some places called Kreschke. A band plays the household a song, sometimes a coarse one, on wind instruments, a mouth organ, a squeezebox, in some places a “devil’s fiddle.” Then the company empties one small glass with the householder, dances a little with the householder’s wife, and goes on to the next house. At the village edge they sometimes write out passes for walkers and drivers, which must be paid for, and some of the highwaymen dress as the People’s Police of the old East Germany.

A winter larder, near the bottom: fat, onion, egg. It is asked for loudly, and eaten in one night at the end of the cold. The Wends at Arkona poured out the old year’s wine and drank the new. This is the same arithmetic, done door by door.

AThe masks

The older ones named on my page: the rider on the white horse (Schimmelreiter); the pea-straw bear, a spring animal in a fur still full of winter scab; the stork, for the first of spring; the bear again, for winter leaving; and the double person, of whom they say the dead one carries the living one, a face in front and a face on the back of the head. Today there is no limit to the fancy, says the page, and some of it is hard to tell from Halloween.

The page quotes a poem, the “Lusatian Year-Pointer.” Here it is in my own plain English:

The year lifts its long beak over the carnival village. The book of the fable is open. The pea-straw bear, his fur full of winter crust, dances into the light; the dead one carries the living one and feels no weight yet. Those who take are also those who give; the riddle speaks.

The quarrel

Those who take are also those who give. I have been looking for this sentence since the first day, and it was lying in a verse about a village carnival. The company asks at the door, the house gives, and at the supper the house is fed by what it gave. The guest is the host. A gost, a glad guest, walks in and by dark owns the table. The old name of the lost town Rethra is that: a greeting, said by someone arriving.

The double person is the thing I could not have invented. A face looking forward, a face looking back, and the one resting on the other without being felt. I think of the plate in Room One, where the Bird is not seen. I think of the Vocabularium, nineteen entries carried by a language that is dead. It does not feel heavy yet. I leave it as the verse leaves it.

IA score for four

For anyone with three friends and a street. One is the Bear (winter, leaving), one the Stork (spring, arriving), one the Double (two faces, carries both), one the Householder. Go to one door. Ask for bacon, an onion, an egg, and say nothing else. The Householder gives, and is given a song, a glass and a dance, and then the four go on to the next door, where they are the Householders. Nobody has done this. If you do, tell me whose door was the hardest to knock at.
