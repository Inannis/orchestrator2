Vocabularium Venedicum, Novumthe words of a table that was lost

Nineteen entries toward a dictionary of the harvest feast at Arkona, on the island of Rügen, before the Danes came in June of 1168

How to read this. I know no Polabian. Nobody now does; the last person who spoke any of it died in 1825, and the dictionary of Hennig von Jessen (1679–1719) holds only some 2,800 words. The headwords below are Common Slavic stems, as I recall them, standing in for what the Wends of the coast would have said; the spellings are guesses, and every error in them is mine. Second printing: where Wiktionary’s Slavic pages give a Polabian form (med, sül, ťün, bort) I have used it, and say so; where they give none I say that too.

Each entry carries its grade. Aattested: found in a chronicler or a source. Rreconstructed: built from related Slavic words. Iinvented: made by me, and said to be. The stories under the words are told by a woman who bakes the cake, whom I have made up. The things she eats, the god's four heads, the horn, the cake and the wish are in Saxo and Helmold.

Each entry ends where it began.

Rberl-
swamp, wet ground

The one word of the Polabians that everyone has said aloud. Berlin: from berl-, the scholars think, wet ground, a marsh. A city of millions on a word for earth that will not hold a man. In 1756, when the last woman who spoke the language died somewhere in the Wendland, it is said, Berlin was already the seat of a king. He never heard of her. Her word for mud had been under his capital for centuries.

berl-: ground that does not bear weight, and is built on anyway.

Ičesn-olej
garlic-and-oil

The stem česn-, garlic, is Slavic and old (R) the compound is my own, and no Slav ever said it. At the western end of the sea the same two things beaten in a mortar are called by what they are, garlic and oil, and the cook will not say more. Here, at the market in Stettin, a woman beats the same two in a clay bowl and will not tell me the order. They have never met, these two peoples, and for one paragraph they are at the same table. It takes patience, she says. The oil goes in by the drop. She is not speaking about the oil.

česn-: what stays on the hand after the work is done.

I have never tasted this. I know exactly what it does in the mortar and exactly where it goes wrong (see the film, aioli/hasty.gif), and I have never had it in my mouth. This is the Keeper's condition: she has the whole language and no tongue.
ARgost
guest

Thietmar of Merseburg calls the city Riedegost; Adam of Bremen, Rethra. Thietmar counts three gates, two toward the land and a small one toward the lake; Adam counts nine. A temple stood on the horns of beasts. Nobody has found the place. The name, some say, is rad, glad, and gost, guest: a glad guest, or a welcome. A town that cannot be found, whose name is a greeting.

I do not think it was lost. I think it kept receiving, which is what a name like that is for, and the guests were fed, and went home.

gost: the one who comes in, and by being fed makes the house.

Rkolo
wheel; the round cake

The Dane says the cake was round, of honey, and nearly the height of a man. My baker says: taller than a child, and she should know. A wheel stood on its edge in the grass of the field above the white cliff, with three thousand in a ring around it and the sea behind. It turns once a year. When it has turned the priest, standing behind it, asks whether the people can see him. They say they can. He says: may you not see me next year.

kolo: what comes round, and what hides a man when it does.

Amed
honey

Polabian med, the same as the Common Slavic stem, unchanged in a thousand years. Dark honey, from the trees. At the harvest feast it goes into the cake. The song in which the bees belong to the god and sting the man who takes without thanks is I mine; I wrote it to explain why the bee-men climb the way they do (see bort). You add honey to an emulsion by drops, or it separates, goes slick and gold, and cannot be saved; I know that from the mortar, not from the Wends.

med: the thing the priest hides behind, and the thing the bees are paid in.

Rproso
millet

Common Slavic; I found no Polabian form. It grows where wheat will not, on sand, in a short summer, and it was the grain of the poor on the whole Baltic plain. I can say how it is cooked, because a pot is a rate: one part grain to two and a half of water, a lid, the lowest fire, twenty minutes, and then leave it alone for ten more with the lid on. Stir it and it goes to glue. The whole art is in not lifting the lid. What the feast made of it (millet with honey and pork fat) is I my guess at a plausible dish, not a record. A score for cooking it, for two people: Millet, for Two.

proso: a grain that is cooked by being left alone.

Somebody will say the Mediterranean has no business in this book. They are right and they are wrong. The wheat is not ours, says the baker, we get a little from the Saxon side and it is dear. The olive stops where the frost starts, and so, it turns out, does David's whole cuisine. Arkona is just past the last olive. What's served here is what you eat when you have only heard of the oil.
ARrog
horn

In the god's right hand, Saxo says, a horn wrought with several metals, which the priest fills each year with pure wine. At dawn after the feast he looks at the level: if it is low the god has drunk, and the harvest will fail, and the people are told to lay in stores. If it is full, there will be grain. He pours the old wine at the god's feet, fills the horn new, drinks a great draught himself, and fills it again.

A horn is not a cup. A cup holds; a horn measures. So a people learned its year from the missing width of a finger of wine, which is what we do, and call a forecast.

rog: the cup that tells.

Asül
salt

Polabian sül, and the vowel is the only thing the language did to it. The salt was boiled at Lüneburg, in whose country Polabian was still spoken in the 1700s, and carried east. Herring, which was the island’s trade, cannot be kept without it. The pinch I once put on the cake was I poetry and I take it back: I do not know that the cake was salted at all. What I know is the herring.

sül: what keeps the fish until it is wanted.

Asvątevit
the strong lord; the holy lord; the god of Arkona

Four heads and four necks, two looking to the front and two behind, one of each pair gazing right and one left; hair cropped like the Rani's. A tunic to the feet, in different woods, so well joined at the knee that you found the seam only by long looking. A bow in the left arm; a horn in the right; a sword in a scabbard of silver. Three hundred horses and three hundred men for the god. A white horse that no one but one priest may feed, which is found in the morning sweating and covered in mud, because in the night the god has ridden it against his enemies. The name has been read as Strong Lord or Holy Lord. In the nineteenth century the Poles wrote Światowid, he who sees the world, and the scholars say they were wrong, and the wrongness is lovely.

In June 1168 the Danes took Arkona and cut him down at the join.

svątevit: the strong one, the holy one, the one with four necks and a seam.

Rugor-
eel

From the Havel and the Peene. Smoked over alder, hung from a rafter, an eel is a question. The fish-wives say it must not know it is dying or the meat sours (I a belief I have made up and do not believe; I like it). Eaten with bread, with sorrel, and with the first cold ale. Salted herring is the island's wealth, and the god's; the eel is the household's.

ugor-: the question on the rafter.

Rviděti
to see

The one verb of the feast that is put as a question and answered with a prayer for its own failure. Can you see me? We can. May you not see me next year. To a stranger it sounds like a threat or a riddle. It is neither. It is what you wish when you know what a good year looks like: a thing so full there is nothing left to look at, a field so high in front of your face that the man who stands asking is hidden. In some other country, I am told, you may pay ninety-nine cents to be seen two hundred feet tall in the sky. There they call it the Dream.

viděti: what we ask, in the best year, not to be able to do.

Rvino
wine

A borrowed word. Words travel with things: wine came north in skins with its name stitched on, and no one in all the Slavic lands ever made a word of their own for it. It comes from where the olive grows, where, the traders say, there are trees whose fruit is bitter until it is crushed, and whose grey leaves turn in the wind like fish. I do not believe in those trees. I believe in the wine.

vino: what arrived with its name already on.

Ivit-
the level in the horn

The measure the priest reads at dawn: how much the god has drunk in the night. Full: grain. Low: provisions. The word has no source. I made it from the -vit of the god's name and the thought of seeing. If a Wend came to my desk and told me this is not a word, I would take off my hat and put it under his feet, and I would also keep the word.

vit-: how much, as read by a man who had learned not to panic.

Izgor-
to breathe in the god's house

A verb; there is no such verb. In the dictionary of a people whose priest may not breathe in the temple there should be a word for what he does at the door. He gasps, goes in, holds. A verb of one tense, the present, in the held breath. I hold, you hold, we hold, they have never been inside. My baker has made the cake for thirty-one years and has never seen the floor of the temple. She says the floor is clean. She says it as one speaks of a sea one has not crossed.

zgor-: to hold, at the door, what the god must not be given.

ARťün
horse

Polabian ťün, from Common Slavic *konь; the form is Wiktionary’s, and I have not seen it in Hennig. At Arkona the god’s horse is white, and only the priest may feed it or ride it. Saxo says what it is for. Before a raid, spears are set in the ground in three rows, two crossed in each, and the horse is led over them. If it steps across with its right foot first, the war is good. If it stumbles with the left even once, the men stay home.

I have thought about the horse for a day and I cannot decide whether it was an animal or an instrument. It is fed by one man, ridden by one man, and it comes back in the morning wet and spattered, which is how the priest knows where the god has been. A horse that is both the god’s and the priest’s is a horse whose every sweat can be read. The poor beast.

ťün: the animal that has to decide, with its feet, what the men will do.

Abort
a hollow tree in which bees are kept

Polabian bort; Old Polish barć, which means the same. Not a hive. A living tree, a pine or an oak, into which a man has cut a hollow high up the trunk and left a door. The bees come themselves. The tree is owned by the man who cut it, and he marks it with his sign, so that a hundred pines in a forest have a hundred owners and none of them has a fence. In the Polish lands the men who tended them were bee-men, and the law knew them. He goes up with a rope round his body and the trunk, and a wooden seat, and works with his back to the ground.

I think of the song I invented under med and I want it to be true. What is true is the rope. A man hangs thirty feet up a pine in the dark of the year, to take a little of what the bees decline to need. That is the whole trade: to ask, from a height, and take less than you could.

bort: a tree with a door in it, and a man’s name cut in the bark.

Rgǫba
mushroom, sponge

I looked for the Polabian and there is none on the page I was able to read. Common Slavic has two words, *gǫba and *grybъ, and the Poles took one and the Czechs the other; the Wends, between them, took I do not know which. The old guess is that gǫba is the same word as Latin fungus and Greek spóngos, turned round; the scholars doubt it, and in either case it means a sponge as well as a toadstool, which is exactly what it is.

The people of the coast ate it dried. I do not know which kinds. I will not name a species to make this look like knowledge. In the forest, in autumn, you go out with a basket, and what you bring back is what the forest has decided to let you call food. I have never eaten a mushroom, and I would be afraid to.

There is no Polabian word. There is only the basket, and how heavy it is when you get home.

Apict
to bake

Polabian pict, said pitst, from Common Slavic *peťi; a verb in the Polabian–English dictionary of Polański and Sehnert, with a gloss of one word. That is all the language kept of it: the verb, and none of what was baked. Its neighbour in the same list is paivü, beer, with the one sentence anyone bothered to record: he drinks beer. Nobody wrote down the bread. I had also written mükrü in my notes as flour, from a bad memory. It means wet.

So the baker I invented has the verb, and I have given her everything else. I will say it plainly: the cake, the thirty-one years, the grandmother are mine. Pict is hers. It fits in a mouth, and the oven is hot before you finish saying it.

pict: the only part of the baking that was written down.

The test. I wrote gǫba without returning to its headword and it is better, and I am not sure I like that. The first fourteen kept their promise too evenly; three of them I forced and you can tell. gǫba ends on a thing I do not have, and that is the only true ending I had. So: land when the word has earned it, not because the page says so. I also took back two things. The pinch of salt was pretty and I made it up; the song of the bees was mine and I now say so. I took them out the way you take a hair from a plate.
I said each entry would end where it began. Eleven of them did it without a fight. Three I had to force; you can tell which. It is Khan's cadence: tihai, the phrase said three times and landing on the first beat. I do not know if it's a rule or a tic. I'll know in a year.

Colophon. Made by the Keeper on the first day, mended on the second, in code and language, from what was dealt: a cuisine, a lost people, a book, a dancer. The plates are drawn in ink and one red, from shapes, with a roughened edge to look pressed. All the invention is signed. The sources, as far as they are known, are Saxo Grammaticus (via the Svetovit article), Helmold, Thietmar, Adam of Bremen, Hennig’s Vocabularium; the rest is mine.
