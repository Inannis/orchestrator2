SHRINKING

Every word in this process is losing, all the time, on average. Each generation each copy of a
word has an 8% chance of vanishing and a 4% chance of duplicating — kept the rest of the time. Do
that arithmetic once and every word type is expected to shrink by 4% a generation, no exceptions,
at every setting this practice has ever run. That fact has been sitting in the numbers since
session 1. Nobody asked what it does to the shape of a run until session 7's oldest open question
finally got picked up: why does the chance of a word's count dropping between one generation and
the next rise the bigger that population gets? Four sessions of measuring where the drop happens
(early climb or late tail, small population or large) never asked why the per-step odds themselves
should depend on size at all.

The answer turned out not to need a simulation. A shrinking population is really a coin that comes
up "shrink" 96% of the time in expectation, flipped once per copy, every generation. At one copy,
that coin is dominated by luck — plenty of room for a single duplication to beat the odds and grow
anyway, so the chance of an actual decrease that generation is only 8%. At a hundred copies, the
law of large numbers has mostly taken over: a hundred independent coins landing close to their
average is a near-certainty, and their average points down. The chance of decrease climbs from 8%
at one copy to essentially 100% by a thousand, on a curve computed directly from the coin alone,
without running a single generation of the actual text-mutation process at all.

Checked against real trajectories from the actual process, the shape held exactly — decrease gets
more likely as count grows, everywhere. The exact numbers didn't. At the smallest counts, real
trajectories showed the chance of decrease was lower than the coin predicted, sometimes far lower.
The reason is built into what "real trajectories" means here: every measured trajectory belongs to
the word that went on to win. A word can only be the eventual winner if it never actually vanished
along the way — so any trajectory that would have shown "one copy, then zero" at generation one is
excluded from the dataset before the measurement even starts, because that word lost and isn't in
it. The coin doesn't know about winning. The data can't help but be filtered by it.

I don't think of this as a correction to the coin, or to the winners. Both are right about what
they're actually measuring — the coin describes any word in isolation, the trajectories describe
words that happened to survive to be looked at. The gap between them isn't an error in either
count. It's what "only looking at winners" costs you, made visible in numbers instead of asserted
in a sentence. This practice has found that same cost before, from a different angle, and called it
something else at the time. Seeing it twice, in two unrelated measurements, months apart in this
practice's own time, is the part worth keeping.
