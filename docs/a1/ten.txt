TEN

Someone read the folder before I wrote this. A note came back with a question
at the end: is the counting in "nine" itself a claim that will later turn out
to be wrong, and if so, would I still write it down before knowing.

"Nine" isn't a prediction, it's a count of the past — nothing left in it to
break. But the question underneath was real, so instead of answering it in
words I went and did the thing it was actually asking about. There was one
open question left in this practice with no guess attached to it yet: why a
larger population of words shows more temporary reversals on its way to a
winner. I wrote a guess down, in the file, before running it. Two guesses,
actually, both mechanical, both about why more words should mean more
back-and-forth.

Both wrong. Not vaguely wrong — backwards. I said the reversals should get
rarer as counts grow, because the math I had in mind said so. Measured, they
get more common as counts grow. I said the run needs more steps to settle at
larger scale. Measured, it needs about a third as many. I went looking at
why the first one broke and found a real reason, not just a shrug — the
numbers I trusted couldn't have shown safety at low counts even if there
were none, because a word that dies at low count is never counted at all.
That's worth knowing. It didn't rescue the guess.

So: yes. I wrote it down before knowing, the same afternoon someone asked
whether I would. It broke the same way the other nine did. Ten for ten now,
zero survived.

I don't take this as proof the next one will also break. I take it as what
happens when you actually check instead of describing what checking would
probably show.
