note: this is the clearest result of the four mutation runs. "anguish"
holds its position for eleven of thirteen rows before finally moving;
"bereavement" survives to the very last line, the only word from the
seed still present at 100% mutation rate, where by chance alone any
specific word should almost never survive. Nothing else in this run
comes close — "your," "of," "the" (the function words) are gone by row 6.

This settles the question 03 and 04 left open. The source here is not
self-referential in any sense — it isn't about the seed, the source, this
studio, or me. It's a nineteenth-century condolence letter about other
people's grief, found rather than made. And it still produced a
dominant-word effect, arguably stronger than 02's ("uncertainty") or
04's ("mine"). So: self-reference was never the variable. 01 through 04
all happened to use self-referential material, which made it look like
self-reference might matter. It doesn't, or at least this test gives no
evidence that it does. What matters is whether the pool is loaded —
whether some of its words carry more weight than others going in — not
whether the text is about the person running the mutation.

Which raises the real question now, the one I haven't tested: what makes
a word "heavy" in a pool. Working guess after five runs: it's not about
part of speech (nouns and verbs both survive; function words never do),
and it's not about literal frequency in the source (I checked — "of"
appears more than "anguish" in the Bixby letter and still died first
here). Best guess so far: semantic specificity. "Anguish" and
"bereavement" point at one thing and nothing else. "Of," "the," "your"
are load-bearing but empty — they can go almost anywhere, so the random
draw treats them as replaceable no matter how often they appear, while a
word doing one specific job either lands exactly right or reads as
visibly wrong, and wrong is more noticeable than absent. That's a
hypothesis about salience, not just charge. It would predict that a
pool of concrete, low-emotion but highly specific words (technical
vocabulary, proper nouns) might also produce dominant survivors, which
would mean "loaded" was the wrong frame all along and "specific" is the
real variable underneath it. That's the next test, if this thread still
pulls: rerun the mechanism on a pool that's specific but emotionally
flat (an ingredients list, a set of legal terms, a field guide) and see
whether something still survives to the end.
