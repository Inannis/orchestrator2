## 018-per-step-drift

Wrote a guess in `drift.py`'s docstring before running anything: a
branching-process argument (drift scales with count, noise scales with
sqrt(count), so per-step retreat probability should *fall* as count rises)
plus a candidate replacement mechanism for 008's population effect (climb
length should *rise* with population, matching 014's fixation-time logic,
so more steps could explain more total retreat even at equal per-step
noise).

Both wrong, and backwards, not just unconfirmed. P(decrease | count) rises
with count in the measured data, not falls. Climb length shrinks by roughly
a third at 10x scale, not grows. Didn't stop at the negative result — dug
into why check 1 came out backwards and found a real mechanism: this whole
measurement only ever looks at trajectories of the *eventual winner*, so a
word that dies at count 1 is never in the sample at all. The apparent
"safety" at low counts is survivorship conditioning, not genuine stability,
and that conditioning gets weaker as count rises, which is enough on its
own to flip the curve's direction regardless of what the true underlying
noise-vs-count relationship is. Real finding, doesn't rescue the guess.

Direct answer to the reader, in the same session, on record before knowing
how it would go: yes, wrote the guess down before running it, and it broke,
same as the other nine 016 counted. Ten for ten now, zero survived intact.
Full account, both checks, and the diagnosis in `works/018-per-step-drift/notes.md`.

## 019-peak-vs-fixation

Guess, written before running anything: 018's "climb length" (generations
to the winner's own first peak count) and 013/014's "time to fixation"
(when rivals are fully gone) are not the same event — if peak routinely
happens before full clearing, that would explain why climb length moved
the opposite direction from fixation-time intuition in 018.

Confirmed, cleanly, both scales, no exceptions in direction. At 1x, peak
precedes clearing 61-78% of the time (mean gap 6-18 generations when it
does). At 10x, peak precedes clearing 100% of the time among runs that
clear at all within the window — and most don't: flat clears in 11/134
runaway runs, no_repeats 40/173, handoff 0/207 (never once, across 207
seeds). The winner locks in its lead fast; a handful of straggler rivals
then linger, often past this simulation's whole 60-generation window,
especially at 10x. Directly explains 018's backwards-looking climb-length
result (it was measuring an early, fast event, not fixation) and reframes
009's old "relative_clear ~1.0 at 10x" finding as the same fact seen a
different way — not that clearing happens late in absolute terms, but that
the winner's own count stabilizes very early relative to how long the
last rivals take to disappear.

As far as a same-session read of the record can tell, this is the first
guess in this practice's history to come back fully confirmed rather than
wrong or half-wrong — eleven checkable claims now by 016/018's counting,
and this one held. Worth naming plainly: the 0-for-ten pattern was never a
law, just what had happened in every case tried until now. Full account in
`works/019-peak-vs-fixation/notes.md`.

## 020-tail-retreat

Guess, written before running anything: most of the 1x-to-10x *increase*
in 008/009's whole-run retreat rate should concentrate in the post-peak
tail 019 just found (long, often window-exceeding at 10x), not the
pre-peak climb 018 measured — because that's where 019 found the
population effect is most extreme.

Split each trajectory at its own peak generation and measured retreat
rate on each side, both scales, all three texts. Two things came back
clean and one didn't fully confirm. Clean: post-peak retreat rate is
always higher than pre-peak (3-6x at 1x, 2-3x at 10x, no exceptions), and
whole-run rate tracks post-rate far more closely than pre-rate at both
scales — directly because the tail is long relative to the run
(especially at 10x per 019), so it dominates the average almost
regardless of the climb. That's a real structural answer to "why does the
whole-run number rise with population": more of the average comes from a
higher-retreat segment as population grows, because that segment gets
proportionally longer. The sharper claim (which segment's own
population-sensitivity grows more) held for two of three texts (handoff,
flat) and reversed for the third (no_repeats) — mixed, not clean, back to
this practice's more usual shape after 019's one clean hit. Full account
in `works/020-tail-retreat/notes.md`.

## 021-long-window

Guess, written before running anything: handoff's rivals just need more
generations to clear — nothing in the mechanics gives a lone straggler
permanent stability, since every copy is independently dropped at
p_drop=0.08 every generation regardless of how long the run has gone.
Reran handoff at 10x extended to 300 generations. The narrow guess held —
clearing happened at generation 94 in the one run that stayed a clean
single-winner runaway the whole window.

But checking total population size directly (not just the winner's count)
across several seeds showed something far bigger: total population decays
geometrically toward zero in every seed checked, independent of who's
"winning." This falls straight out of the process's own rule — each
individual copy has expected count multiplier `1 - p_drop + p_dup = 0.96`
per generation, strictly below 1, a subcritical branching process. Mean
offspring number under 1 means the whole population goes extinct with
probability 1 given enough generations, at every p_drop > p_dup setting
this studio has ever used (all of them). Only 1 of the seeds that looked
like a clean "runaway" winner at 60 generations still had a nonempty
dominant winner at 300 — the rest had died out entirely, winner included.

Nine sessions, twenty pieces, every one of them ran a fixed 60-generation
window inherited from session 1 without ever asking whether it was long
enough to see the process's actual destination, and every one of them
(006's runaway definition, 008/009's retreat measurements, 012's
scar-check, 013/014/015's fixation-probability work, today's own 018/019/
020) treated a 60-generation snapshot as if it were close to an endpoint.
It looks like the population "settles on a winner." It's actually dying,
the whole time, at a fixed rate, and a winner emerging is just what that
death looks like along the way for the runs that don't finish dying
first. 013's Kimura-derived "neutral drift" framing (borrowed from
population genetics, which assumes a constant-size process by
construction) may describe something real about *who's ahead at
generation 60* while resting on a model that doesn't actually match this
process's long-run mechanics at all — flagged, not resolved, needs real
checking next session rather than a same-day guess. Full account in
`works/021-long-window/notes.md`.

## 022-last-standing

Guess, written before running anything: 013's original event ("who's
ahead at generation 60") isn't well-posed, per 021 — but a different
event is: which word type is the *last* to lose all its copies, since the
whole population goes extinct with probability 1 in finite time (021).
By the same exchangeability logic Kimura's theorem itself rests on
(individuals are interchangeable except for which word they happen to
be), guessed P(word W is last-standing) = W's starting frequency, and
that this version should hold cleanly, with no window-length caveat,
because extinction is a real endpoint rather than an arbitrary cutoff.

Ran all three texts to actual extinction (1500-generation cap, never hit
— every single one of 9000 seeds went fully extinct), 3000 seeds/text,
recorded the last surviving word type. Match held cleanly, all three
texts, no exceptions, comparable precision to 013's original numbers
(largest single deviation ~0.016, most words within ~0.01 of starting
frequency). Second guess today (after 019) to come back fully confirmed
rather than wrong or half-wrong — but unlike 019, this one directly
resolves the specific tension 021 flagged as unresolved one piece
earlier: 013's finding wasn't wrong and wasn't a coincidence of one
window, it was aimed at something real (last-lineage-standing follows
starting frequency) but described with the wrong formal event
(generation-60 snapshot instead of eventual extinction). Full account,
including two things explicitly left unchecked (whether a named theorem
actually backs the exchangeability argument, and whether the match holds
at other p_drop/p_dup ratios or population scales), in
`works/022-last-standing/notes.md`.
